The Surprisingly Squid-like Nature of Internet Routing (and My Failing Attempt at a Metaphor)

Not just any squids, mind you, but squid routers (yes, I know, I made that up). This got me thinking about redistribution protocols, specifically, and how incredibly… well, squid-like they really are.

Redistribution protocols are the glue that holds different routing domains together. Think of it like this: you've got your local network (your little corner of the internet), using one protocol – let's say OSPF, because it sounds fancy. Then your ISP uses a completely different protocol, maybe BGP, to connect to the wider internet. How do these two disparate worlds talk to each other? That’s where redistribution comes in. It’s the octopus-tentacle-like bridge that translates the language of one protocol into the language of another, allowing data to flow seamlessly (ideally).

Now, back to my Squid Router analogy (I’m strangely attached to it now). Our giant squid router – let’s call him Inky – has to make some tough choices. He's got a mountain of routing information coming in from all these different protocols. Some are trustworthy, others… less so. Some routes are faster, some are cheaper. Inky has to decide which routes to advertise to the other parts of his internet kingdom, and which to keep under wraps. This is where things get tricky. How does Inky prioritize? How does he avoid creating routing loops – that dreaded black hole where data goes to die?

It’s not unlike the real world, is it? Think about political alliances, or even just planning a family reunion. You’ve got competing priorities, different information sources, and a whole lot of potential for things to go spectacularly wrong if you're not careful. (My last family reunion involved a near-riot over the seating arrangements. Routing protocols, I suspect, are slightly less chaotic, but only slightly.)

And here's where my initial enthusiasm for the squid metaphor starts to wobble. Because squids aren't exactly known for their elegant decision-making processes. They’re more… instinctual. Are redistribution protocols truly this simplistic? Probably not. There's a lot of sophisticated filtering, summarization, and metric manipulation involved. It’s more like a highly organized, slightly neurotic squid orchestra, where each instrument (routing protocol) plays its part in a complex symphony of data transmission. Much more elegant, but less visually appealing than a giant squid, I’ll admit.

But the core concept remains. These protocols are bridging the gap between different networks, making the seemingly impossible task of global internet connectivity possible. And that's something worth appreciating, even if my attempt to explain it using a fictional giant squid wasn’t the most polished illustration.
